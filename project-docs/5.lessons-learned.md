# Lessons Learned

## Overview

This project helped me practice Agile Scrum concepts by planning a beauty product-discovery experience across the Skincare, Haircare, and Makeup product areas.

Through creating the Product Backlog, estimating user stories, planning two sprints, organizing work in Asana, and completing a Sprint Review and Retrospective, I learned how Agile planning supports structured and customer-focused product decisions.

## 1. Customer Needs Should Drive Product Features

Creating personas before writing user stories helped me focus on specific customer needs rather than creating general beauty-product features.

For example:

- Emma, a busy working mom with dry skin, needed a quick and simple skincare routine.
- Alex, a tour guide working in humid conditions, needed haircare products that help control frizz.
- Olivia, a makeup enthusiast with oily skin, needed foundation options that provide suitable coverage and long-lasting wear.

This showed me that personas are useful because they connect product decisions to real customer goals, problems, and contexts.

## 2. Clear User Stories Improve Planning

Writing user stories in the format “As a..., I want..., so that...” helped clarify who the feature is for, what the customer needs, and why the feature matters.

Adding acceptance criteria also made each user story more specific and easier to evaluate. The acceptance criteria identified what must be true for a story to be considered complete.

For example, the Skin Concern Selection story required customers to select one or more concerns, receive relevant recommendations, see an explanation of why products matched their concerns, and refine the results.

## 3. Prioritization Requires Both Value and Effort

I learned that user stories should not be prioritized only by how large or complex they are.

The Product Backlog considered both:

- Customer and business value
- Story-point estimates
- Sprint capacity
- Dependencies and available product information

The highest-value features were selected for Sprint 1 because they supported important customer needs, including dry-skin routine recommendations, skincare-concern selection, and oily-skin foundation filtering.

This project showed me that a lower-effort task is not always the highest priority, and a high-value task may need to be delayed if important information or dependencies are not ready.

## 4. Story Points Are Relative Estimates

Story points helped me compare the relative effort, complexity, uncertainty, and risk of the user stories.

For example:

- Minimalist Skincare was estimated at 5 story points.
- Skin Concern Selection was estimated at 8 story points.
- Inspiration-Based Product Suggestions was estimated at 13 story points because it involved image uploads, identifying makeup categories, and generating product suggestions.

I learned that story points are not estimates of hours. They are used to compare work and support sprint planning based on the amount of effort a team can reasonably take on.

## 5. Sprint Capacity Helps Create Realistic Plans

The sprint capacity was 25 story points for both Sprint 1 and Sprint 2.

Sprint 1 included 21 assigned story points, while Sprint 2 included 23 assigned story points. Keeping the planned work below the 25-point capacity helped create a more realistic plan and left room for uncertainty.

I learned that sprint planning should consider capacity rather than simply placing as many high-value stories as possible into one sprint. A sprint plan should balance value with the amount of work that can realistically be completed.

## 6. Dependencies Can Affect Sprint Completion

The Foundation for Oily Skin story was selected for Sprint 1 because it had high customer and business value. However, it could not be completed because the required foundation product details still needed to be collected and connected to the filtering criteria.

This taught me that a high-priority feature can still be blocked by missing information, product data, or other dependencies.

In future planning, I would identify dependencies earlier and confirm that the required product data, requirements, and information are available before committing a story to a sprint.

## 7. Retrospectives Support Continuous Improvement

The Sprint Retrospective helped identify what worked well, what could be improved, and what actions could be taken in the next sprint.

The retrospective showed the importance of reviewing completed and incomplete work without treating the process as a failure. Instead, it created an opportunity to understand why work was delayed and how planning could improve.

The Foundation for Oily Skin story can be reviewed, refined if necessary, and returned to the Product Backlog for consideration in a future sprint. Incomplete backlog items should be re-evaluated and planned again based on current priorities and remaining work.

## 8. Visual Project Tracking Makes Work Easier to Understand

Using an Asana Board made it easier to organize user stories by workflow stage:

- To Do
- In Progress
- Review
- Done

The Asana Dashboard also made it easier to view task status, work completion, assigned story points, and progress over time.

I learned that visual project-management tools help teams and stakeholders quickly understand project progress, identify blocked work, and review sprint status.

## Conclusion

This project improved my understanding of Agile product planning, backlog prioritization, user-story writing, acceptance criteria, story-point estimation, sprint capacity, and retrospective improvement.

In future projects, I would spend more time identifying dependencies before sprint planning, breaking larger user stories into smaller deliverable pieces, and using feedback from completed sprints to improve future estimates and backlog priorities.
